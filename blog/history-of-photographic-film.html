The History of Photographic Film and Formats

From dangerous chemicals on glass plates to the iconic 35mm canister, explore the physical history of photography.

History • ⏳ 12 min read

When you digitize a family photo album using a modern scanner and our PhotoSplit Studio app, it's easy to take for granted the incredible physical technology that made those prints possible. The photograph you hold in your hand is the result of over 150 years of relentless chemical engineering and mechanical innovation.

The Pre-Film Era: Daguerreotypes to Glass Plates

The story of capturing light permanently begins long before flexible plastic film existed. In 1839, Louis Daguerre introduced the Daguerreotype. This process involved a highly polished silver-plated copper sheet treated with iodine vapor to make it light-sensitive. The exposure times were brutally long—sometimes requiring subjects to sit perfectly still in a neck brace for several minutes.

By the 1850s, the Collodion Process (Wet Plate) took over. Photographers used glass plates coated with a syrupy chemical mixture. The catch? The plate had to be coated, exposed, and developed before the chemical dried (about 15 minutes). This meant photographers had to carry a portable darkroom tent with them everywhere they went, including battlefields during the American Civil War.

The turning point arrived in the 1870s with the invention of the Dry Plate. A gelatin emulsion allowed glass plates to be prepared in advance and developed later, freeing photographers from the portable darkroom and dramatically speeding up exposure times.

The Birth of Flexible Film: George Eastman's Revolution

The real revolution for amateur photography occurred in the 1880s, driven largely by George Eastman, founder of Kodak. Eastman realized that replacing heavy, fragile glass plates with a flexible roll would democratize photography.

In 1889, Eastman introduced the first commercially successful transparent roll film. It was made of nitrocellulose, which was highly flammable (a major hazard in early cinemas and archives). Despite the fire risk, this flexible film allowed the creation of the Kodak camera, marketed with the famous slogan: "You press the button, we do the rest." Consumers could shoot 100 pictures, mail the entire camera back to Rochester, New York, and receive their developed prints along with a reloaded camera.

The Reign of 35mm

While various roll film formats existed, none became as ubiquitous as 35mm. Interestingly, 35mm was originally designed for motion picture cinema, not still photography. In 1913, an engineer named Oskar Barnack at the Leitz optics company (later Leica) wanted a small, portable camera for testing cinema film exposures. He turned the 35mm cinema film sideways, creating the 24x36mm frame size we know today.

The resulting Leica I camera, released commercially in 1925, sparked the modern era of photojournalism and street photography. The 35mm format perfectly balanced portability with image quality, and by the mid-20th century, it was the undisputed standard for both professionals and amateurs. The film was later moved to a safer "safety film" base (cellulose acetate), eliminating the fire hazards of nitrate.

Medium Format and Large Format

While 35mm was popular for its portability, professionals who needed massive enlargements for magazines or gallery prints stuck to larger formats.

  • Medium Format (120 film): Introduced by Kodak in 1901 for the Brownie No. 2, 120 film is much wider than 35mm. It is famously used in Hasselblad cameras (the cameras that went to the moon).
  • Large Format (4x5 or 8x10 inch sheet film): Used primarily for landscape and architectural photography, these massive single sheets of film capture an astonishing amount of detail that even modern 100-megapixel digital cameras struggle to match perfectly.

The Instamatic and Polaroid Innovations

In the 1960s and 70s, companies sought to make photography even easier. Kodak introduced the 126 cartridge (Instamatic) and later the tiny 110 cartridge (Pocket Instamatic), which required no threading—just drop it in and shoot. While the image quality was lower than 35mm due to the smaller negative size, their convenience made them wildly popular.

Meanwhile, Edwin Land's Polaroid Corporation perfected Instant Film. By combining the film, developer, and fixing chemicals into a single physical unit that processed itself immediately upon ejection from the camera, Polaroid created a cultural phenomenon that remains popular today.

Conclusion

Today, the vast majority of new photos are taken on smartphone sensors and saved as digital files. However, billions of physical frames of history remain trapped on the film formats discussed above. Understanding the physical medium of these photos—whether it's the high-resolution detail of a medium format negative or the fading dyes of a 1970s Polaroid—is crucial for proper digitization and preservation. When you use tools like a flatbed scanner and our PhotoSplit application, you are participating in the final step of a technological journey that began nearly two centuries ago.
